Testing, not proof. The rules below were fixed before the results came in, so they can't be tuned to make a pattern appear. A spike match only means two things happened within 48 hours of each other. Lulls are flagged in the 0–48 hour and 2–7 day windows after the lull. Earthquakes are tested both ways, plus 20-year historical tests of upticks and downticks.

Solar wind spikes

Source: NOAA SWPC real-time solar wind, 1-minute proton speed from whichever spacecraft is active. It's reduced to hourly medians, and hours with fewer than 20 good minutes are skipped. The record before this logger started (Nov 18, 2026 onward) comes from the RealAether Cosmic Ray Detector, which logs the same feed.

Spike rule: an hour whose median speed is at least 100 km/s above the median of the hours 30 to 6 hours before it (a 24-hour baseline ending 6 hours earlier), or an hour that crosses up through 500 km/s. A new spike needs 12 hours since the previous spike start.

Volcano alert changes

Source: USGS Hazard Notification System (HANS). Each Volcano Activity Notice or VONA is read for its current and previous Alert Level and Aviation Color Code, and logged once if either changed. A snapshot of all monitored U.S. volcanoes is compared run to run as a safety net. The time used is the notice's sent time.

Solar wind lulls

The live log also records lulls, using the same definitions as the 20-year downtick test (thresholds frozen from that study, not retuned on the short live record):

  • Speed drop: the first hour whose 24-hour change in hourly median speed is −100 km/s or more (the same 100 km/s size as the upward spike rule; also about the 5th percentile of 24-hour changes in the 20-year ACE record). A new speed drop needs 12 hours since the previous one, and the condition has to start fresh.
  • Dynamic-pressure drop: the first hour whose 24-hour change in proton dynamic pressure, P = 1.67×10⁻⁶ n V² nPa, is at or below −1.4212 nPa (the 5th percentile of those 24-hour changes in the 20-year record). Same 12-hour refractory rule. Density comes from the same NOAA SWPC real-time solar wind feed; hours back-filled from the Cosmic Ray Detector have speed only, so pressure drops can be logged only after density is on the record.
  • Storm-end lull: the first 3-hour Kp of 2 or less after the current episode has reached Kp of 5 or more (G1+). The live log uses NOAA SWPC’s 3-hour planetary K-index (the same Kp scale). The 20-year test used definitive GFZ Kp.
  • Charge then release: a speed drop whose mean speed over the 72 hours ending 24 hours earlier (the three days before the drop day itself) is at or above 464.9 km/s, the 75th percentile of those 72-hour means in the 20-year record. It is a tag on a speed drop, not a separate event, so the same hour is not counted twice.

Geological flags (fixed in advance): each lull is checked in two windows after it, the same ones used in the 20-year test: 0–48 hours and 48–168 hours (days 2–7). A window is only scored once it has fully elapsed. A lull is flagged in a window if any of these happened in that window:

  • an earthquake of M6 or larger, or
  • a U.S. volcano alert level or aviation color going up (the logger’s stand-in for an eruption; this is an alert change, not a verified eruption catalogue), or
  • an M4.5+ cluster: the declustered M4.5+ count in that window has a one-sided Poisson p below 0.05 against the 20-year mean rate of 12.47 declustered quakes per 48 hours (about 20 or more in 48 hours, or about 41 or more in days 2–7).

Worldwide M4.5+ quakes happen every few hours, so a flag is reserved for a real uptick (or an M6+, or a raised volcano alert), not for the everyday background. The live record is still short, so the running flag counts will jump around; the 20-year test is the one to read for whether downticks come before more quakes.

Matches and chance

An alert change is a match if a spike start falls within ±48 hours of it. Only changes inside the solar wind record are tested. Changes from before the record are listed but not counted.

Chance: the share of the record that sits within ±48 h of any spike is the probability that a randomly timed alert change would match. Expected matches = tested changes × that share. The p-value is a one-sided binomial tail. Alert changes at the same volcano are not independent, so treat the p-value as rough. Only a long record that beats chance by a wide margin would be worth a closer look, and even that would not show cause.

Earthquakes

Source: USGS ComCat via the FDSN event service: every earthquake of magnitude 4.5 or larger worldwide (the size the global network records fairly completely). Each run re-reads the last three days so magnitude revisions are picked up. Quakes of M6 or larger are highlighted.

Aftershocks. One big quake can bring hundreds of aftershocks, which would swamp any count. We report two versions: raw and declustered. Declustering uses the Gardner & Knopoff (1974) space–time windows: working from the largest quake down, any smaller quake within its window (for example about 500 days and 53 km after an M6, or about 990 days and 94 km after an M8) is treated as part of its sequence and removed. It is a blunt, standard tool; it also removes some independent quakes, which is fine for a comparison like this one.

Live test (fixed in advance): for every solar wind spike (same rule as above) with a full 48 hours after it, count the M4.5+ quakes in the 48 hours after the spike start. Expected = the logger's average quake rate × 48 h × number of spikes. The p-value is a one-sided Poisson tail. The live record is short, so it is mainly a running log.

20-year test

To get a meaningful answer now instead of in a few years, we ran the same test on public archives from January 2006 to November 2026:

  • Solar wind and IMF: NOAA SWPC real-time ACE hourly averages (monthly files, the same NOAA real-time stream family the logger uses). Hours flagged as bad are skipped.
  • Geomagnetic storms: GFZ Potsdam Kp index (Matzka et al. 2021, kp.gfz.de). A storm start is the first 3-hour Kp of 5 or more (G1+), with 12 hours between starts.
  • Southward IMF: the first hour with Bz ≤ −10 nT, with 12 hours between starts.
  • Earthquakes: USGS ComCat, M4.5+ worldwide (about 155,000 quakes).

Superposed epoch analysis: we line up every trigger (spike, storm start or southward-IMF start) and count quakes in the 48 hours after each one, then average. Null: we slide the whole set of triggers by one random offset (wrapping around the 20-year record), which keeps their spacing and clustering, and repeat 10,000 times. The p-value is the share of random shifts that give at least the observed count. The main test, chosen before running anything, is declustered M4.5+ quakes after solar wind spikes. The other 11 rows (raw counts, M6+, seismic energy, storms, southward IMF) are side tests; with 12 tests, a single p below 0.05 is expected by luck about half the time, so the bar for "worth a closer look" is p < 0.0042 (Bonferroni). Seismic energy uses log₁₀E = 1.5M + 4.8 (joules) and is dominated by a few giant quakes.

Context: large studies have looked for links between earthquakes and solar or geomagnetic activity and found no robust, repeatable effect; the few positive reports have not held up under independent tests with declustered catalogs. Our test is a public check, not new physics.

Not used: the Hailo AI hat. It runs an image model for the Cosmic Ray Detector's hit pictures; there is nothing for it to look at here. This is plain statistics.

Results file: quake_baseline.json. Code: quake_baseline.py (Python + NumPy), rebuilt by hand when the archives grow.

20-year test: downticks and lulls

A follow-up, also fixed before looking at the quake associations, asked whether drops and the quiet after a high-activity period come before more earthquakes — a weather analogy (energy builds up, then a lull or release). Same archives, same Gardner–Knopoff declustering, same 10,000 circular-shift permutation test, January 2006 to November 2026. The primary outcome is again the declustered M4.5+ count. Four triggers (speed drop, dynamic-pressure drop, storm-end lull, charge-then-release) times two windows (0–48 hours and days 2–7) give eight tests. The bar is p < 0.00625 (0.05 ÷ 8).

Main result: after 920 speed drops of at least 100 km/s in 24 hours there were 12.52 declustered M4.5+ quakes per 48 hours, versus 12.47 at random times (ratio 1.004, p = 0.38). The closest of the eight was storm-end lulls in days 2–7 (31.75 vs 31.16, ratio 1.019, p = 0.093). None passed the bar. A sensitivity check on M6+ counts, not included in the correction, also found no pass. Dst (a storm-recovery index) was not in the cached inputs, so storm recovery was defined from Kp only.

Volcanoes: the logger’s volcano list is the live USGS HANS alert-level changes, only a handful of recent notices. That is far too sparse for a 20-year superposed-epoch test, and no historical eruption catalogue was on hand, so volcanoes were not included in the historical test. The live log still flags a raised U.S. volcano alert after a lull, going forward.

Results file: quake_downticks.json. Code: quake_downticks.py (Python + NumPy), run on the same ACE, Kp and USGS archives as the spike test.
