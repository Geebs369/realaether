Timeline · 27 November 2026

A single, cited timeline of known meteor and comet impacts and airbursts and extreme solar and CME events. Every entry is marked confirmed, debated, or unverified, and every entry has a source. The crater list for the last 100,000 years, plus a few famous older structures such as Chicxulub, Meteor Crater and Popigai, comes from the Earth Impact Database as compiled on Wikipedia, because the PASSC database site was down when this page was built. Historical airbursts, NASA CNEOS fireball statistics, the October 2022 Taurid lunar flashes, Encke node-crossing calculations, and the Younger Dryas verdict come from the public record and from our Impact Hypothesis studies. Solar-particle (Miyake) events and geomagnetic storms, with Dst and Kp where we have them, follow those same studies and NOAA / Kyoto WDC / SILSO records.

This is not a claim that one flood, one impact, or one flare explains the ancient world. It is a scoreboard of what is dated and how strong the evidence is. The companion flood-myths section lives on the Impact page.

  1. Vredefort Confirmed

    2,023 ± 4 million years ago · Impact · crater · Free State, South Africa · 160 km crater

    Earth’s largest confirmed impact structure, and one of the oldest. The original crater has been deeply eroded; the remaining dome is a UNESCO World Heritage site.

    • Earth Impact Database, via Wikipedia, List of impact structures on Earth (compilation as of 2024)
    • Kamo et al. ages as compiled there (2,023 ± 4 Ma); diameter 160 km

  2. Sudbury Basin Confirmed

    1,849 million years ago · Impact · crater · Ontario, Canada · 130 km crater

    One of the largest confirmed impact structures, later deformed by tectonics. The basin is a major nickel-copper mining district.

    • Earth Impact Database, via Wikipedia, List of impact structures on Earth (2024 compilation): 130 km, 1,849 Ma

  3. Manicouagan Confirmed

    215.56 ± 0.05 million years ago · Impact · crater · Quebec, Canada · 100 km crater

    A large, well-preserved Late Triassic impact. A ring-shaped reservoir now fills the eroded inner basin. Links to a Late Triassic turnover have been proposed and remain less definitive than the K–Pg case.

    • Earth Impact Database, via Wikipedia, List of impact structures on Earth (2024 compilation): 100 km, 215.56 ± 0.05 Ma

  4. Chicxulub Confirmed

    66.051 ± 0.031 million years ago · Impact · crater · Yucatán, Mexico · 150 km crater

    The impact most widely accepted as the cause of the Cretaceous–Paleogene mass extinction, including the non-avian dinosaurs. A roughly 10 km asteroid or comet struck shallow tropical seas and produced a global debris and fire-pulse layer.

    • Earth Impact Database, via Wikipedia, List of impact structures on Earth (2024 compilation): 150 km, 66.051 ± 0.031 Ma
    • Alvarez et al. 1980, Science 208:1095; Schulte et al. 2010, Science 327:1214

  5. Popigai Confirmed

    • Earth Impact Database, via Wikipedia, List of impact structures on Earth (2024 compilation): 100 km, 35.7 ± 0.2 Ma

  31. Younger Dryas boundary — impact trigger? Debated

    about 12,900 years ago (≈ 10,900 BC) · Our study · verdict · global climate event; claimed North American impact

    The Younger Dryas was a sudden Northern Hemisphere cold snap in the middle of the warming that ended the last ice age, not the end of the ice age itself. A proposed impact or airburst trigger (the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis, supported in public by Randall Carlson among others) is probably not the cause: our review puts confidence at about 85% against. There is no crater of the right age, the nanodiamond, platinum and melt-glass evidence is disputed, and our Taurid / Encke node-crossing test is neutral (nearest crossing 649 years away; a random date does as well, p ≈ 0.53). Meltwater from glacial Lake Agassiz weakening Atlantic overturning is the better-supported explanation. A small airburst cannot be completely ruled out.

    • RealAether Monument–sky study (Impact Hypothesis, Part 3), 27 Nov 2026
    • Encke node-crossing test: nearest crossing 649 yr from the YDB, p(random date as close) = 0.54

  35. Miyake event, 7176 BC Confirmed

    7176 BC · Solar · miyake · recorded in ¹⁴C / ¹⁰Be (global)

    A confirmed extreme solar-particle event: a sharp, nearly global spike in radiocarbon (¹⁴C) and beryllium-10 (¹⁰Be). Events like this do not burn the land; before the electrical age the main human effect would have been great auroras. They recur at ordinary solar-activity levels, with a lower-bound rate of about 2–10% per century.

    • Confirmed Holocene Miyake events as compiled in RealAether cataclysm study (Part 2), 27 Nov 2026
    • Discovery and annual ¹⁴C / ¹⁰Be literature, including Miyake et al. 2012, Nature 486:240 (AD 774/5 prototype); later work extended the list to 7176 BC, 5259 BC and 664–663 BC

  53. Miyake event, AD 774/5 Confirmed

    AD 774/5 · Solar · miyake · recorded in ¹⁴C / ¹⁰Be (global)

    The strongest confirmed Miyake event of the Holocene, first identified as a rapid ¹⁴C rise in Japanese cedar rings and later confirmed in ¹⁰Be ice-core records. Models find only modest ozone loss and no significant climate effect; the ground-level radiation dose was small. Chronicles of unusual auroras are the main human record.

  58. Carrington event Confirmed

    1–2 September 1859 · Solar · geomagnetic · Earth (global auroras; telegraph systems) · Dst about −850 to −1,760 (estimated) · Kp pre-dates the Kp index

    The benchmark extreme geomagnetic storm of the instrumental era, following a white-light solar flare observed by Richard Carrington and Richard Hodgson on 1 September 1859. Telegraph systems sparked and shocked operators. Estimated Dst ranges from about −850 nT (low-end) to −1,760 nT. From the two or three Carrington-class storms since 1859, we estimate about 11–16% probability per decade (90% CI 2–37%).

    • Carrington 1859; later Dst reconstructions compiled in our Part 2 study (low-end about −850 nT; mid-range often about −1,200 nT)
    • Riley 2012, Space Weather 10:S02012 (about 12% per decade)

  59. February 1872 storm (probable Carrington-class) Debated

    February 1872 · Solar · geomagnetic · Earth (global auroras; telegraph era)

    A severe 19th-century storm, probably of Carrington class according to Hayakawa et al. (2023). Counting it with 1859 and 1921 is what raises the per-decade odds from about 11% (two events) to about 16% (three events) in our Part 2 study.

    • Hayakawa et al. 2023, as cited in RealAether cataclysm study (Part 2)

  60. Tunguska Confirmed

    30 June 1908 · Impact · airburst · Podkamennaya Tunguska, Siberia, Russia · airburst, roughly 50–60 m object; ~2,150 km² of forest flattened

    The largest impact event in recorded history: an object roughly 50–60 m across exploded in the air over a remote region of Siberia and flattened about 80 million trees across some 2,150 km². Few or no deaths were confirmed. There is no crater of that event.

    • Classic surveys of the 1908 Tunguska airburst; energy and size in the commonly cited 50–60 m / multi-megaton range (see also NASEM 2022 context in our Part 2 study)

  61. May 1921 storm Confirmed

    May 1921 · Solar · geomagnetic · Earth (telegraph and railway fires, including New York) · Dst −907 ± 132 (Love et al. 2019) · Kp pre-dates a complete modern Kp record

    A Carrington-class geomagnetic storm. Telegraph and railway infrastructure caught fire, including in New York. Love et al. (2019) estimate Dst at −907 ± 132 nT.

    • Love et al. 2019, Space Weather (Dst −907 ± 132 nT)
    • RealAether cataclysm study (Part 2)

  62. Curuçá River (“Brazil’s Tunguska”) Debated

    13 August 1930 (reports) · Impact · airburst · Curuçá River, Amazonas, Brazil

    Newspaper and later accounts describe a possible airburst that scorched forest along the Curuçá River. Details are thin, and the event is much less well documented than Tunguska or Chelyabinsk.

    • Later discussions of the 13 August 1930 Curuçá event (sometimes called “Brazil’s Tunguska”); documentation is sparse, so this page marks it debated

  63. Sikhote-Alin Confirmed

    12 February 1947 · Impact · fall · Sikhote-Alin mountains, Russia · iron fall; more than 100 craters, the largest tens of metres

    A witnessed iron-meteorite fall. The meteoroid broke up in the atmosphere and rained down tons of fragments, leaving more than 100 small craters. It is a fall, not a city-scale airburst.

    • Classic documentation of the 12 February 1947 Sikhote-Alin iron meteorite shower and crater field

  64. March 1989 Québec blackout Confirmed

    13–14 March 1989 · Solar · geomagnetic · Québec, Canada, and the wider Earth system · Dst -589 nT · Kp 9 · 1989-03-14 01:00 (OMNI2)

    A severe geomagnetic storm that collapsed the Hydro-Québec grid, leaving about 6 million people without power for roughly nine hours. In the OMNI2 hourly Dst record we used (1963–2026), this is the strongest storm: Dst −589 nT on 14 March 1989 at 01:00. Kp reached 9.

    • NASA OMNI2 hourly Dst (and Kyoto WDC Dst): −589 nT at 1989-03-14 01:00, from our Part 2 storm list
    • NOAA / Kp = 9; Hydro-Québec blackout accounts

  65. Halloween storms Confirmed

    28–30 October 2003 · Solar · geomagnetic · Earth (including a Malmö, Sweden blackout and airline reroutes) · Dst -383 nT · Kp 9 · 2003-10-30 22:00 (OMNI2)

    A famous series of extreme solar eruptions near solar maximum of Cycle 23. Kp reached 9. In our OMNI2 record, Dst reached −383 nT on 30 October 2003 at 22:00. A Malmö blackout and airline reroutes are documented.

    • NASA OMNI2 / Kyoto WDC Dst: −383 nT at 2003-10-30 22:00; NOAA Kp = 9

  66. 20 November 2003 superstorm Confirmed

    20 November 2003 · Solar · geomagnetic · Earth · Dst -422 nT · 2003-11-20 20:00 (OMNI2)

    A second extreme storm only weeks after the Halloween events, and the second-strongest in our 1963–2026 OMNI2 Dst list: −422 nT on 20 November 2003 at 20:00.

    • NASA OMNI2 / Kyoto WDC Dst: −422 nT at 2003-11-20 20:00, RealAether Part 2 storm list

  67. Carancas Confirmed

    15 September 2007 · Impact · fall · near Carancas, Puno, Peru · stony meteorite; ~13 m crater

    A stony meteorite reached the ground and excavated a crater about 13 m across near a village. Some locals reported feeling sick afterward; a shock-heated, water-filled crater and arsenic-bearing sediments have been discussed as possible causes, rather than “meteorite poisoning.”

    • Tancredi et al. and follow-up work on the 15 September 2007 Carancas crater, Peru (~13 m)

  68. Chelyabinsk Confirmed

    15 February 2013 · Impact · airburst · Chelyabinsk, Russia · ~20 m stony object; airburst ~440 kt TNT

    A rock about 20 m across entered over the southern Urals and exploded in the air. NASA’s official energy estimate is about 440 kilotons of TNT (roughly 30 times the Hiroshima bomb). The shock wave blew out windows across thousands of buildings and injured about 1,500 people, mostly from flying glass. It was not seen coming in advance.

    • NASA / CNEOS fireball record; Brown et al. 2013, Nature 503:238; Popova et al. 2013, Science 342:1069
    • Our CNEOS window 1 Jan 2005–4 Oct 2026 includes Chelyabinsk; 81 fireballs ≥1 kt, 3.72 per year (RealAether Part 2)

  69. CNEOS fireballs ≥1 kt Confirmed

    1 Jan 2005 – 4 Oct 2026 (survey window) · Impact · fireball stats · Earth (global bolide detections) · 81 events ≥1 kt; mean 3.72 per year

    NASA CNEOS fireball data in our study window show 81 events of at least 1 kiloton, or 3.72 per year, with a power-law index of 0.98 (90% CI 0.82–1.20). That matches Brown et al. (2002) almost exactly (3.7 per year, index 0.90). The largest event in the window is about 441 kt (Chelyabinsk). This is a survey statistic, not a single impact.

    • NASA CNEOS fireballs; RealAether cataclysm study (Part 2), future_risk.json, window 2005-01-01 to 2026-10-04
    • Brown et al. 2002, Nature 420:294

  70. February 2022 Starlink loss Confirmed

    3–4 February 2022 · Solar · cme · low Earth orbit (Starlink launch cohort) · Dst did not reach −100 nT in our OMNI2 ≤ −100 nT storm list · modest storm, large operational effect

    A relatively modest geomagnetic storm — it does not appear among OMNI2 storms of Dst ≤ −100 nT in the 1963–2026 list we used — still led to the loss of 38 newly launched Starlink satellites, which re-entered after atmospheric density rose at their unusually low deployment altitude. It is a reminder that space systems can fail in storms well below Carrington or even Québec 1989 strength.

    • SpaceX and NOAA accounts of the 3–4 February 2022 Starlink losses (38 satellites)
    • RealAether Part 2: the event is listed among documented technological harms; it is absent from our OMNI2 Dst ≤ −100 nT catalogue, so the storm was weaker than that threshold

  71. Taurid lunar impact flashes Confirmed

    29–31 October 2022 · Impact · lunar · near side of the Moon (NELIOTA survey) · meteoroids roughly 20–250 g

    The NELIOTA lunar impact-flash survey recorded 16 flashes from Taurid meteoroids on 29–31 October 2022, a year predicted in advance as a Taurid resonant-swarm return. Across August 2019–August 2023, flashes per observed hour were 1.84 times higher in October–November than in other months (p = 0.0005). Sporadic flashes show no such excess (p = 0.40). This is the one clear positive in our Taurid tests.

    • Liakos et al. 2024 (NELIOTA); RealAether Taurid study, 27 Nov 2026

  72. Gannon storm Confirmed

    10–12 May 2024 · Solar · geomagnetic · Earth (G5 level; GPS disruption to farm equipment among the effects) · Dst -406 nT · Kp 9 · 2024-05-11 02:00 (OMNI2)

    The first G5 (extreme) geomagnetic storm of Solar Cycle 25, often called the Gannon storm. In our OMNI2 record, Dst reached −406 nT on 11 May 2024 at 02:00, the third-strongest storm in the 1963–2026 hourly list after March 1989 and 20 November 2003. Kp reached 9. Documented effects included GPS disruption to farm equipment. We found no deaths attributed to the storm.

    • NASA OMNI2 / Kyoto WDC Dst: −406 nT at 2024-05-11 02:00; NOAA SWPC Kp = 9, G5

  73. Solar Cycle 25 maximum Confirmed

    October 2024 (smoothed sunspot-number peak) · Solar · cycle · the Sun · smoothed sunspot number 160.9

    NASA, NOAA and the Solar Cycle Prediction Panel announced on 15 October 2024 that the Sun had entered the solar-maximum period of Cycle 25, noting that the exact peak month would be identified only later. SILSO later placed the 13-month smoothed sunspot-number maximum in October 2024 at 160.9 — stronger than the 2019 panel’s midpoint forecast of 115. By April 2026 the smoothed number had fallen to 88.6. NOAA SWPC has projected a minimum around December 2030.

    • NASA/NOAA joint solar-maximum announcement, 15 October 2024
    • SIDC/SILSO: smoothed monthly sunspot number peaked at 160.9 in October 2024; RealAether Part 2 (value 88.6 by April 2026)

Methods

How the list was built

  • Craters. Confirmed impact structures of the last ~100,000 years, plus Vredefort, Sudbury, Manicouagan, Chicxulub, Popigai, Chesapeake Bay, Nördlinger Ries, El’gygytgyn, Tenoumer, Pingualuit and Bosumtwi, are taken from the Wikipedia list of impact structures on Earth, which compiles the Earth Impact Database (EID) as of 2024. The EID’s usual host (passc.net / impact.uwo.ca) was unavailable when this page was assembled, so the Wikipedia compilation is the source we could actually open. Ages, diameters and coordinates follow that table. Rio Cuarto and Aorounga’s age, and the Australasian tektite field (source crater undiscovered), are marked debated where the compilation itself flags uncertainty. Tenoumer’s listed age is 1.6 ± 0.1 Ma; earlier papers also discussed a much younger date.
  • Historical impacts and airbursts. Tunguska (1908), Curuçá (1930), Sikhote-Alin (1947), Carancas (2007) and Chelyabinsk (2013) are entered from the modern record. Curuçá is marked debated because the documentation is thin. The Ch’ing-yang (1490) stone shower is marked unverified: chronicles describe it, but there is no confirming crater or meteorite study on the same footing as the modern events.
  • Our studies. The 29–31 October 2022 Taurid lunar flashes (NELIOTA; Liakos et al. 2024) and the October–November rate test are from the Taurid study. NASA CNEOS fireballs (≥1 kt, 1 Jan 2005–4 Oct 2026: 81 events, 3.72 per year) and the Miyake / geomagnetic analysis are from the cataclysm study (Part 2). Encke node-crossing years and the Younger Dryas verdict (probably not an impact trigger, about 85% confidence; nearest Encke crossing 649 years away, p ≈ 0.53 versus a random date) are from the Taurid and monument–sky studies. Node crossings are marked “model”: they are calculated orbital geometry, not dated impacts.
  • Solar events. Confirmed Miyake events are 7176 BC, 5259 BC, 664–663 BC, AD 774/5 (the strongest of the Holocene) and AD 993/4, with ¹⁴C and ¹⁰Be records (Miyake et al. 2012, 2013; Mekhaldi et al. 2015; later annual-ring work). The ~12,350 BC event is a candidate (Bard et al. 2023) and is marked debated. Geomagnetic storms use NASA OMNI2 hourly Dst, which draws on the Kyoto World Data Center, for 1963–2026: March 1989 Dst −589 nT (Kp 9), 30 October 2003 Dst −383 nT (Kp 9), 20 November 2003 Dst −422 nT, and the May 2024 Gannon storm Dst −406 nT (Kp 9, NOAA SWPC G5). Carrington 1859 and May 1921 pre-date that Dst series; 1921 is given as Dst −907 ± 132 nT (Love et al. 2019). The February 2022 Starlink loss (38 satellites) followed a modest storm that did not reach Dst ≤ −100 nT in our catalogue. Solar Cycle 25’s 13-month smoothed sunspot-number peak is October 2024 at 160.9 (SIDC/SILSO), after NASA and NOAA announced the maximum period on 15 October 2024.
  • Evidence labels. Confirmed means a widely accepted structure or event with physical or instrumental records. Debated means the event or structure is real, or seriously proposed, but the cause, age, or interpretation is disputed. Unverified means a historical claim without confirming physical evidence. Model means a calculated epoch, not a dated impact.
  • What this page is not. It is not an impact-risk product — NASA Sentry is. It does not argue for a single global flood or a lost civilization. No home address or local network information is published here.

The machine-readable file behind this page is events.json. Reports: Taurid, cataclysm, monument–sky, long-period comets and Centaurs.
