Earth's spin axis wobbles like a spinning top. The Sun and Moon pull on the bulge around Earth's equator, trying to stand the tilted planet upright. Because Earth is spinning, it doesn't straighten; instead the axis slowly swings around in a cone. The north celestial pole, the point the axis aims at, traces a circle about 47° across (radius ≈ 23.4°) around the pole of Earth's orbit, taking about 25,800 years per turn. Along the way it passes a series of pole stars. Polaris is only the current one.

Why it wobbles

Earth isn't a perfect ball: it bulges about 21 km at the equator, and its axis is tilted 23.4°. The Moon and Sun pull harder on the near side of that bulge, a twisting force (torque) that tries to line the equator up with them. A spinning top answers a sideways push by swinging its axis around instead of falling over, and Earth does the same. The Moon supplies about two-thirds of the effect and the Sun one-third. The planets add a slow tilt of Earth's orbit itself.

The same motion slides the March-equinox point west along the stars by about 50″ a year (one degree every ~72 years): the "precession of the equinoxes," noticed by Hipparchus around 130 BC.

Earth's magnetic field and day length through the eras

Magnetic field. The coloured lines are published reconstructions of Earth's dipole moment from magnetized lava, pottery and lake and sea sediments: GGF100k (Panovska, Constable & Korte 2018), CALS10k.2 and HFM.OL1.A1 (Constable, Korte & Panovska 2016), and SHA.DIF.14k (Pavón-Carrasco et al. 2014). Where they disagree, that is the real uncertainty. White is measured: IGRF-14 shows the dipole weaker in 2025 than in 1900 and falling about per century right now. The future is unknown. The field comes from churning molten iron in the core, and nobody can forecast it centuries ahead: it rises and falls, and excursions or reversals come without a schedule. The dashed line is only a straight-line illustration of today's trend, not a prediction. The field has nothing to do with precession; it just shares the timeline here.

Day length. The Moon's tides act as a brake, so Earth's spin slows and the day lengthens. Ancient eclipse records give about +1.8 ms per century on average (Stephenson, Morrison & Hohenkerk 2016): about 2.3 ms from tidal braking, partly offset by Earth's crust rebounding since the ice age. Over a whole precession cycle that adds up to less than half a second per day. The figures are shown relative to today and assume the long-term average rate holds. Tidal slowing doesn't weaken the magnetic field: the geodynamo is driven by heat escaping the core, not by how fast Earth spins.

All 19 pole-star eras

Each slice of the clock belongs to whichever bright star (magnitude 4.0 or brighter) is nearest the pole. An era starts the year that star takes over from the previous one, the moment the two are equally far from the pole. Some eras have no good pole star at all: the nearest bright star stays more than 5° away. Each era lists notable objects in the stretch of sky the pole crosses.

Zodiac ages (secondary)

The same wobble also moves the March-equinox Sun (the "vernal point") backward through the zodiac. Which constellation it is "in" depends on the definition, and there is no official definition of an astrological age. Below are two: the official IAU constellation boundaries (drawn in 1930 by Delporte, fixed to the stars in the 1875 frame), where the constellations have very different widths and the path also crosses Ophiuchus, and equal 30° signs fixed to the stars using the Lahiri convention (India's official sidereal standard: ayanamsa 23°15′00.658″ on 21 March 1956).

Method & uncertainty

  • Precession model. Vondrák, Capitaine & Wallace (2011), "New precession expressions, valid for long time intervals" (A&A 534, A22), as implemented in ERFA (the open version of the IAU SOFA library). It is designed for ±200,000 years and matches the IAU 2006 model near the present. The authors checked it against long numerical integrations; over ±13,000 years the remaining differences shift the dates on this page by only a few years.
  • Pole stars. Positions and motions from the HYG 4.1 catalogue (Hipparcos-based). Each star is moved through space (proper motion, distance and radial velocity) to every date, and its distance from the mean pole of date is measured. The pole's 18.6-year nodding (nutation, ≤ 9″) is ignored.
  • Era boundaries are where the nearest star of magnitude ≤ 4.0 changes, found on a 2-year grid. Because stars approach the pole slowly, a boundary year is good to roughly ±10–50 years; don't read the last digit literally.
  • Zodiac. IAU boundaries tested with Astropy's constellation lookup (Delporte/Roman tables, B1875), with the vernal point refined to 0.1 year. The boundaries are fixed lines, so star motion does not enter. Equal signs: the ecliptic longitude of a fixed Lahiri zero point measured in the ecliptic of date. A different anchor shifts every date by the same amount (Fagan-Bradley: about 63 years earlier). Treat zodiac dates as ±5 years.
  • Magnetic field. Dipole moments come straight from the model files: GGF100k and CALS10k.2 publish dipole-moment series, and for SHA.DIF.14k, HFM.OL1.A1 and IGRF-14 we compute the dipole moment from the first three Gauss coefficients (4π a³/μ₀ × √(g₁₀² + g₁₁² + h₁₁²)). CALS10k.2 and HFM.OL1.A1 are used only for their published range, 8000 BC–AD 1990. Era values are the mean (and range) over each era. Future eras are left unknown on purpose.
  • Day length. Δ(length of day) = 1.78 ms/century × (year − 2000)/100, from Stephenson, Morrison & Hohenkerk (2016, Proc. R. Soc. A 472: 20160404), shown at each era's midpoint. Decade-scale wobbles of ±a few ms are ignored.
  • Points of interest. Positions from SIMBAD (CDS, Strasbourg). Each object is assigned to the era during which the pole passes closest to it (within 20°).
  • Years. Calculations use astronomical year numbering (year 0 = 1 BC); dates are shown as BC/AD. Dates are March equinoxes. Refraction, extinction and the exact time of the equinox are ignored in the dawn view, so it is a guide, not a reconstruction.
  • Code and data. The data file behind this page is precession_data.json; the scripts that built it are in src/ (Python: pyerfa, Astropy, NumPy, SciPy).
